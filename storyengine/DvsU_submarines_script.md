# Every US Submarine Class Ever Built (2026)

**Thesis:** The US Navy kept building submarines to survive being found, until nuclear power let them stop being found at all.

## Act 1 — The submarine begins as a coastal ambush weapon, not a fleet asset

**1. USS Holland (SS-1)** · 117 words

Theodore Roosevelt asked the Navy for a harbor-defense craft in 1898, not a fleet weapon — the same limited role France gave its submarines against Britain. John Holland got there by trading the rival Plunger's steam plant for a 45-horsepower gasoline engine and sixty lead-acid cells, shrinking the hull to 54 feet at the cost of a surface deck so low it left the crew almost no shelter from the sea. The Navy bought Holland in 1900 doubting her wartime value — then Admiral Dewey testified two of these craft at Manila Bay would have made his victory untenable. Sold for scrap in 1913, she was rescued three years later and displayed not as a warship but as art.

**2. A-class (Adder class)** · 120 words

None of the Adder-class submarines could cross the Pacific under their own power. When the Navy sent them to defend Manila, they went as deck cargo, unloaded off the collier Caesar like any other freight — a strange way to deliver an underwater ambush weapon. Inside, they still ran on Holland's gasoline-and-battery layout, just enlarged, with a compensating tank that fixed the trim problem that had sunk Holland's own first trial. That gasoline stayed dangerous: it fouled engine-room air for years, and on 24 July 1917 an explosion and fire aboard A-7 killed her commander, Lt. (jg) Arnold Marcus, the first submarine officer honored with a destroyer's name. The Navy's first production submarine class arrived as cargo and left in flames.

**3. F-class** · 110 words

Twenty-one men died in March 1915 not because an enemy found F-4, but because the vessel's own batteries corroded through the hull that housed them. F-class had been sent to Hawaii the year before as harbor guards — a defensive posting so unglamorous the Navy never bothered explaining what threat it was guarding against. Historians remember F-4 anyway, because raising her wreck sent a diver to 304 feet, a depth record that held for twenty-five years, and because Frank Crilley went back down to free a trapped partner and surfaced with the Medal of Honor instead. The vessel built to watch a harbor is remembered for what happened after it sank.

## Act 2 — The Navy scales the submarine into a long-range fleet scout

**4. S-class** · 119 words

In 1916 the Navy stopped picking one company's design and funded three yards to solve the same problem differently. Electric Boat, Lake, and Portsmouth each built to one 800-to-1,000-ton spec, and the results exposed the trade-offs: S-1 dove in sixty seconds, but torsional vibration from an undersized crankshaft wrecked her engines in trials, while Portsmouth's hull took forty seconds longer to disappear. Even the deck gun retracted breech-first into the hull to shave drag, a fix crews hated and swapped for a plain 4-inch gun. Built for a long Atlantic war against U-boats, the S-class missed it entirely — its fifty-one submarines became the interwar fleet's backbone instead, still too slow for the battle-fleet speed the Navy would soon demand.

**5. Barracuda class (V-1 group)** · 101 words

The speed the S-class couldn't reach became this class's whole assignment: 21 knots surfaced, matched to the battle fleet's pace. Borrowing German war-prize diesels, the Barracuda class chased that number in a bigger hull—2,119 tons surfaced. It still only made 18.7 knots on trials, by secondary accounts, rough in a seaway with balky motors. Odder still: these three submarines rose at Portsmouth, but Electric Boat sat frozen out of naval work for six straight years, kept alive building printing presses and fish-skinning machines instead of vessels. The class that missed its own top speed became the one the Navy learned from.

**6. Porpoise class** · 113 words

Six years after Electric Boat was frozen out of naval work, the Navy handed it half the Porpoise class's FY-34 order — and hedged the other half against it. Two hulls went to Portsmouth, riveted and trusted; two went to Electric Boat, welded and unproven since Cuttlefish. The riveted hulls leaked so badly Porpoise and Pike were pulled to training duty by 1943. The bigger problem hid in the periscopes: the 30-foot scopes borrowed from the V-class were too short, so these submarines broached in moderate seas until a 34-foot fix arrived too late to help them. The Navy's cautious split produced an uncautious answer. Welding won, and every fleet submarine after copied it.

## Act 3 — World War II turns the submarine into America's most effective weapon by accident

**7. Gato class** · 111 words

Porpoise proved welding worked, so the Navy did something almost unheard of next: it took the proven Tambor/Gar hull and froze it, changing only the one flaw that could kill a crew in a single flood — one engine room, now split by a watertight bulkhead and five feet of extra length. Some of the resulting Gato-class submarines then rose a thousand miles from open water, welded together at Manitowoc on Lake Michigan and launched sideways into a river bound for the Mississippi and the sea. They were built to scout ahead of a battle line. Pearl Harbor erased that battle line — and the submarine became America's deadliest weapon anyway, by accident.

**8. Balao class** · 118 words

In late 1941, naval architects Armand Morgan and Andrew McKee worked the fix out by hand, on scrap paper and envelopes: turn the lead ballast a prior class no longer needed into thicker pressure-hull steel. Admiral Cochrane, back from inspecting the wreckage at Pearl Harbor, approved it and added one number — 400 feet of test depth — then had it kept need-to-know. The result, Balao, was barely a new submarine: a Gato hull with heavier skin and a cut-down fairwater, corrected almost by accident rather than designed on purpose. But reused hulls meant reused flaws — gearboxes too fragile for depth-charge shock, vent risers that could flood a submarine — problems the next class would inherit before it could fix them.

**9. Tench class** · 118 words

Balao's shock-fragile gearboxes and flood-prone vent risers were still unresolved when Tench class design began, until an unlikely engineer intervened. Hyman Rickover, years before he became known as father of the nuclear navy, built at the Bureau of Ships a low-speed motor that deleted the reduction gears outright, while Portsmouth moved the ballast tank forward to erase the riser problem and free room for four more torpedoes, 28 in all. The fix arrived late: ready only by spring 1944, most of the roughly two dozen completed vessels never made a war patrol, and Boston alone lost twelve of sixteen contracted hulls to cancellation. A submarine engineered to win the war instead spent decades proving itself after it ended.

## Act 4 — Peacetime forces the submarine to hide better and dive deeper

**10. GUPPY conversion program** · 116 words

The submarines picked for conversion in autumn 1946 weren't tired hulls needing rescue — they were USS Odax and USS Pomodon, Tench-class vessels so new their steel had barely seen war, chosen for GUPPY because they were pristine. The decision inside them was blunt: trade surface speed for speed underwater — streamlining, a snorkel, and a battery dense enough to more than double submerged speed while surface speed fell below 20 knots. The snorkel itself was built against the British Admiralty's explicit advice that the effort was wasted on two-cycle diesels. Framed as a stopgap for the interval before all-new ships arrived, GUPPY instead ran seven variants to 1963 and outlived the fleet it was meant to bridge.

**11. Tang class (SS-563)** · 118 words

The GUPPY conversions were only meant as a stopgap until submarines like this one arrived — instead, they helped end it. Tang class chased the captured Type XXI's underwater performance: a shorter, streamlined hull, test depth raised from 400 to 700 feet, deck guns stripped off. Yet the Navy rejected the German submarine's most impressive feature — hydraulic bow gear that reloaded torpedoes in five minutes with no crew — as too complicated, copying the shape but not the ambition. Even the ambition it kept failed: the new pancake diesels proved so unreliable that three ships needed new engines before their first overhaul. Only six Tang-class submarines were built. Designed to out-German the Germans, it was undone by its own engines.

**12. Barbel class** · 117 words

A contingency plan isn't supposed to become permanent, but Barbel's design assumed it might have to. Engineers borrowed Albacore's teardrop hull and single tapered screw, which pushed all six torpedo tubes forward, then dropped Albacore's troublesome pancake engines for plainer conventional diesels — reliable, if capped near 25 knots. Covert Shores calls the whole class a hedge against nuclear propulsion simply failing. It never had to collect: nuclear submarines proved themselves, so only three Barbel-class vessels were ever commissioned, the last diesel-electric attack submarines the US Navy built. Crews wore an unofficial pin, "Diesel Boats Forever," stitched together in 1969 by ETR3(SS) Leon Figurido aboard Barbel herself. Nuclear crews wore it too. The hedge lost; the pride didn't.

## Act 5 — Nuclear power removes the submarine's only real weakness: time underwater

**13. USS Nautilus (SSN-571)** · 107 words

On 17 January 1955, minutes off the pier, a loose locking pin in Nautilus's starboard reduction gear knocked her onto electric drive; Rickover refused to turn back in front of the watching press, the fix took minutes, and the signal 'Underway on nuclear power' went out regardless. That scare isn't why she's remembered. The Navy had planned a whole class of smaller, slower nuclear submarines to follow her. Then she ran 1,300 submerged miles in 84 hours, ten times any earlier continuous distance, and speed like that got the slower design capped at a handful of vessels instead. The prototype outran the program built to succeed it.

**14. Skate class** · 117 words

Nautilus's 1955 trials doomed the program meant to follow her, and the Navy built it anyway. Carney had approved three attack submarines around the smaller S3W/S4W reactor before Nautilus finished those trials; Watkins's verdict — too slow to avoid detection — arrived after Skate was committed. It shipped, cut to four vessels once the larger S5W took over as the fleet standard. What Skate is remembered for isn't the reactor replaced under her: on 17 March 1959 she surfaced at the North Pole under blue sky to scatter Hubert Wilkins's ashes, having proved a production attack submarine could work the Arctic through winter. Built to set the standard, she became the exception that found where nuclear power could go.

**15. Skipjack class** · 120 words

Nautilus's 1955 trials proved what speed alone could do underwater; Skipjack was approved to prove a submarine could be built fast enough to use it. Its reactor was no stronger than Nautilus's — the gain came from shape, a hull barely 7.6 times as long as it was wide, replacing the elongated form that made WWII fleet submarines unstable at speed. That hull had already been tested — on Albacore, an unarmed submarine running on batteries alone, with no reactor at all. Skipjack paid for its speed in noise, a bill the next class would settle. It never got judged as a fast-attack design alone: before the class finished, one of its own hulls was already being cut open to carry Polaris.

## Act 6 — The submarine becomes a nation's second-strike deterrent, not a warship

**16. George Washington class** · 119 words

That hull, spliced open at the end of the Skipjack line, was Scorpion — stopped under construction at Groton in January 1958 so a 130-foot missile section could be inserted between bow and stern, and renamed George Washington. Rickover, who'd define the nuclear Navy, wasn't asked to help design her: Burke's staff kept him out of the preliminary studies, brought him in only that April, for the reactor plant alone. He pushed the under-ice question anyway and lost — twin reactors for polar work, denied, since none existed yet. Planners wanted the first Polaris submarine ready by 1965. This converted hull sailed her first deterrent patrol in November 1960 — five years ahead of a plan that assumed she wouldn't exist yet.

**17. Lafayette class** · 117 words

Thirty-one hulls became the reserve McNamara wanted to threaten Soviet cities after a first strike — but the Lafayette class went to sea armed with a missile smaller than the one its tubes were built for. The Poseidon C-3 wasn't ready, so vessels deployed instead with Polaris A-2, waiting years for the upgrade already built into their design. That patience is the point: a deterrent doesn't need every submarine finished to spec; it needs enough submarines at sea, armed with something, so the reserve exists before the ideal weapon does. The design traveled too — Britain's Resolution-class submarines mirrored the Lafayette's size and diving planes almost hull for hull, so America's second-strike answer became someone else's deterrent as well.

**18. Ohio class** · 120 words

Lafayette's deterrent force was aging, and better Soviet submarine-hunting made a wider patrol area worth having, so in 1971 the Navy didn't stretch another hull — it started clean. Ohio class carried the answer: 24 missiles, huge resupply hatches, and an overhaul interval stretched past 15 years so vessels stayed hidden longer than they stayed in port. Its new Trident missile was a compromise, built to squeeze into the same launch-tube width that once held Polaris and Poseidon, so the weapon could arm decade-old submarines before Ohio existed. Congress capped the class at 18 vessels, and four of those later gave up their missile tubes for Tomahawks and special-forces lockout chambers — a deterrent submarine finishing its career as a strike submarine.

## Act 7 — The submarine returns to hunting, now permanently invisible

**19. Permit class (Thresher class)** · 115 words

Skipjack's speed had cost it noise, a debt Thresher was engineered to settle — turbines on a sound-isolating raft, a bow sonar sphere, HY-80 steel for depth an enemy's sonar couldn't reach. Four of the class were even laid down as cruise-missile submarines, ordered to carry Regulus II, until Polaris killed that missile in December 1958 and turned them back into hunters before they were finished. None of that is why anyone still says the name today. On 10 April 1963, Thresher went down with 129 aboard during the trial meant to certify the very depth meant to keep her safe — and the survivors were renamed Permit, quieter for having buried the name that built them.

**20. Sturgeon class** · 117 words

Permit's survivors emerged from Thresher's loss quieter; Sturgeon began as barely more than a modest upgrade to that class, until a larger sail for intelligence-gathering masts and Permit's own propulsion in a bigger hull grew it into thirty-seven vessels. The trade was several knots of speed for quiet — the return was a library. Sturgeon crews built and maintained a catalog of Soviet submarine sound signatures, precise enough to pick one vessel out of an ocean at ranges no earlier sonar reached. Commissioned to hunt, the class spent the Cold War on surveillance, strike, and special-warfare work still classified. The Navy's first submarine hid in a harbor. Sturgeon hid everywhere — and stopped needing to be found at all.
