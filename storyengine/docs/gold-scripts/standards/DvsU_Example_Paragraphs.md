## CATEGORY 1: OPENING WITH A HUMAN DETAIL (not the machine name)

**From: Most Hated Fighter Jets (57K views)**

> "Navy test pilot Wally Schirra later described it in his autobiography with a precision that left nothing to interpretation. Lieutenant Commander Edward Feightner, assigned to fly the Cutlass with the Blue Angels in 1951, described a colleague who returned from a test flight, threw his helmet on the ground, and said he quit. The hydraulics failed on Feightner's own demonstration flight at eleven hundred feet — below the minimum ejection altitude. Approximately twenty-eight percent of all Cutlass aircraft produced were destroyed in accidents. Twenty-five pilots were killed. The Navy flew the Cutlass from 1954 to 1959 and never forgave Chance Vought for making them do it."

**Why AI should learn from this paragraph:**
- Opens with a named person, not the aircraft
- Numbers (28%, 25 pilots) arrive AFTER emotional context — they confirm the story, not replace it
- Final sentence ends with institutional irony: "never forgave Chance Vought for making them do it"
- No Wikipedia sentence anywhere
- One paragraph = one idea: the aircraft was so dangerous the Navy hated the manufacturer for it

## CATEGORY 2: OPENING WITH A CONSEQUENCE

**From: Most Hated Fighter Jets (57K views)**

> "James Smith McDonnell, the president of McDonnell Aircraft Corporation, personally wrote to the Navy before production began and warned that the F3H powered by the Westinghouse J40 would be a 'disappointingly underpowered combination.' The Navy overruled him and ordered the aircraft into production anyway. Between 1952 and 1955, eleven Demons crashed. Four pilots were killed. Time magazine called the Westinghouse J40 the worst jet engine in American aviation history. The Navy eventually replaced it with the Allison J71 in the F3H-2, which worked. The manufacturer had been right. The Navy spent four years and four pilots proving it."

**Why AI should learn from this paragraph:**
- Opens with the manufacturer warning the buyer — a concrete human decision, not a machine description
- The thesis is "the manufacturer was right and the institution ignored them"
- Final two sentences close the loop with brutal compression: "The manufacturer had been right. The Navy spent four years and four pilots proving it."
- This paragraph is about the procurement decision, not the aircraft specifications

## CATEGORY 3: OPENING WITH A PARADOX

**From: Every US Battleship Class Ever Built (91K views)**

> "The Maine was not technically a battleship. She was ordered in 1886 as an armored cruiser, reclassified as a second-class battleship in 1892, and destroyed by an explosion in Havana Harbor on February 15, 1898. Two hundred and sixty-six sailors died. The Spanish-American War followed. Whether the explosion was an accident or an act of sabotage was never conclusively established, but yellow journalism and American outrage did not wait for conclusions. 'Remember the Maine' became the rallying cry that pushed a reluctant president into a war that made America a Pacific power. The Maine never fired a shot in anger. She started a war by sinking."

**Why AI should learn from this paragraph:**
- Opens with a contradiction: "was not technically a battleship"
- This immediately creates tension — the viewer has to understand why a non-battleship is in a battleship video
- Final sentence is a perfect closing paradox: "never fired a shot in anger. She started a war by sinking."
- The paragraph is about the decision to go to war — the ship is only the trigger

## CATEGORY 4: OPENING WITH A DATE/EVENT

**From: Every US Warship That Defined the Cold War (our sessions)**

> "On September 2, 1945, the war ended on her deck. The documents were signed in Tokyo Bay, General MacArthur presided, and the teak wood beneath his feet belonged to USS Missouri. Within years she was being decommissioned — the battleship's dominance as the capital ship of the fleet was over. The ship that had seemed obsolete was called back three times before she was finished. But in 1945 she was simply the last punctuation mark of one era and the unmarked first sentence of the next."

**Why AI should learn from this paragraph:**
- Opens with a date and a specific image (the deck, the signing)
- The thesis is about historical transition, not the ship's specifications
- No mention of Missouri's guns, armour, or displacement
- Final sentence ("last punctuation mark of one era... first sentence of the next") delivers the thesis in one image

## CATEGORY 5: FACTS SERVING THE STORY (not replacing it)

**From: Every US Strategic Bomber Ever Built (184K views)**

> "The Boeing B-17 Flying Fortress entered service in 1938 and became the backbone of America's daylight precision bombing campaign over Europe. At 74 feet long with four Wright Cyclone engines producing 1,200 horsepower each, the B-17 could carry 4,500 pounds of bombs at 287 mph to targets 2,000 miles away. Bristling with thirteen .50-caliber machine guns, crews believed the aircraft could defend itself without fighter escort. Between 1938 and 1945, Boeing built 12,731 B-17s across all variants. The Eighth Air Force lost 4,735 B-17s over Europe, a staggering 47% loss rate among heavy bombers. The B-17 proved daylight precision bombing could work. The price was written in blood over German skies."

**Why AI should learn from this paragraph:**
- This is older-style DvsU (more specs) — but notice the specs are selected for a reason
- Every number builds toward the thesis: the precision bombing belief vs. the 47% loss rate
- Final two sentences deliver the verdict: "could work. The price was written in blood."
- Even with more specs than current style, the paragraph avoids Wikipedia tone because the numbers are in service of the thesis

## CATEGORY 6: THE DECISION PARAGRAPH

**From: Every US Warship That Defined the Cold War (our sessions)**

> "Aegis was not a weapon. It was a brain. The AN/SPY-1 phased-array radar that USS Ticonderoga carried when she commissioned in 1983 could simultaneously track more than two hundred targets and engage eighteen of them at once. Previous naval air defence had worked sequentially — find a target, engage it, find the next one. Soviet anti-ship doctrine was built around saturation: send more missiles than the sequential system could handle. Aegis was the answer to saturation. Ticonderoga did not make the carrier invulnerable. She made the calculation of how many missiles it would take to get through a carrier battle group too expensive for the Soviet Navy to consider a reliable option."

**Why AI should learn from this paragraph:**
- Opens with a two-sentence thesis: "Not a weapon. A brain."
- The paragraph is entirely about the doctrine decision, not the ship
- No mention of Ticonderoga's displacement, speed, or hull number significance
- Final sentence describes the strategic effect in terms of the opponent's calculation — not American capability
- This is the model for "the paragraph is about the decision, not the machine"

## COMPARATIVE EXAMPLE: Same unit, two versions

**❌ AI default (catalog):**
"The F-86 Sabre was a transonic jet fighter-bomber developed by North American Aviation and manufactured from 1949 to 1956. It had a wingspan of 37 feet and was powered by a General Electric J47 engine producing 5,910 pounds of thrust. The aircraft could reach a maximum speed of 687 mph. It served during the Korean War as the primary USAF fighter, achieving a claimed kill ratio of 10:1 against the MiG-15."

**✅ DvsU documentary:**
"Buying an F-86 Sabre means buying the aircraft that established American air superiority over Korea, that flew against the MiG-15 in the first jet-versus-jet combat in history, and that broke the sound barrier in a shallow dive. It also means spending between eight hundred thousand and one point two million dollars, finding an engine overhaul facility qualified to work on a General Electric J47, and explaining to your insurance company exactly what you intend to do with a Korean War fighter jet. The Sabre buyer is not optimising for value. They are optimising for the specific experience of flying the aircraft that American pilots flew in 1950 and that shaped every subsequent fighter the United States built for two decades."

**Why the DvsU version is correct:**
- Opens with what the aircraft REPRESENTS, not what it IS
- The "buyer" framing puts the reader inside the decision
- Numbers appear only when they serve the thesis (price = barrier to ownership)
- Final sentence: the aircraft's lasting effect on American fighter design — not its specifications

## STRONG FINAL LINES (collect and learn from these)

These are actual final lines from DvsU scripts. Each one could end the video.

- *"She did not go quietly."* (HMS Warspite — grounded herself rather than be scrapped)
- *"Nobody planned for this outcome."* (MiG-21 — Soviet fighter now owned by American civilians)
- *"Germany withdrew and built the Leopard 2."* (Anglo-German FMBT — brutal compression of a failed alliance)
- *"The war they were built for never came. The fleet built to fight World War III inherited the world instead."* (USS Arleigh Burke)
- *"The manufacturer had been right. The Navy spent four years and four pilots proving it."* (F3H Demon)
- *"The Maine never fired a shot in anger. She started a war by sinking."* (USS Maine)
- *"The answer kept changing because the Soviet tank kept changing. And then the Soviet tank stopped being built entirely."* (FTMA 140mm)
- *"Six prototypes, ten miles per hour, and barely weeks before the war that required them ended."* (A43 Black Prince)
- *"Britain invented the tank in 1916. It now maintains fewer than two hundred of them."* (British tank history)

**Pattern:** Strong final lines are short. They contain a paradox, an irony, or a reversal. They never summarise — they land.

## COMMON AI MISTAKES

These are the errors AI produces by default. All are prohibited.

❌ **Starts with machine name too many times.**
More than 4–5 paragraphs out of 30 opening with the unit name. The AI defaults to this because it is the simplest structure. It must be actively prevented.

❌ **Gives specifications before explaining significance.**
"It had a wingspan of 103 feet and was powered by four engines producing 1,200 horsepower each" — before the viewer understands why any of this matters.

❌ **Ends with retirement date.**
"The aircraft was finally retired in 1978 after 24 years of service." This is a filing cabinet fact, not a closing line. It communicates nothing.

❌ **Writes chronological biographies instead of arguments.**
"It was designed in 1943. It entered service in 1944. It was modified in 1945. It served in Korea. It was retired in 1957." This is a timeline, not a documentary paragraph.

❌ **Uses generic praise.**
"One of the greatest fighters ever built." "A legendary aircraft." "Arguably the most iconic warship of its era." These phrases say nothing and signal that the AI has no specific insight.

❌ **Treats every paragraph with identical narrative weight.**
A pivotal machine (USS Nautilus, P-51 Mustang, Tiger I) and a minor transitional variant should not feel equally important. The writing must reflect the actual historical significance.

❌ **Proves the machine existed.**
"The XB-15 was a large experimental bomber developed by Boeing in the 1930s. It had a 149-foot wingspan." The viewer does not need to be convinced the machine existed. They need to understand why it mattered.

❌ **Adds orphan facts.**
Technical specifications that do not answer: why was it designed this way / what problem did it solve / what consequence did it create. These facts consume words without contributing to the thesis.

❌ **Opens every ACT with a new timeline anchor.**
"In the early Cold War period..." / "By the 1960s..." / "During the Vietnam era..." This turns the video into a textbook chapter structure. Each ACT should open as a new argument, not a new time period.

❌ **Writes a separate conclusion paragraph.**
"So what have we learned from these machines? The history of naval aviation shows us that..." The video ends on the last unit. There is no summary after the last machine.
