# DvsU — Research & Fact Verification Standard v3
## Quality Standard for Automated Documentary Production

---

## CHANNEL IDENTITY

DvsU is not a military channel.
DvsU is not a weapons channel.
DvsU is an engineering documentary channel that uses military machines as its subject.

Strategic frame: **"The Most Controversial / Hated / Failed / Legendary Machines Ever Built"**

The channel is about: engineering decisions, design philosophy, trade-offs, procurement failures, technological evolution, unintended consequences.

War is only the setting. Engineering is the story.

---

## CORE PRINCIPLE

**DvsU does not make exhaustive encyclopedias.**
**DvsU makes curated engineering documentaries.**

When a topic contains more machines than the video can hold, select the machines with the strongest stories — not the most complete list.

The public title may say "Every US Warship Used in Vietnam."
The selection inside must be: "Every US Warship With the Most Defining Engineering Story in Vietnam."

The title stays broad. The selection is curated.

---

## WHY ACCURACY MATTERS

The DvsU audience is 55+ male, serious military history enthusiasts who fact-check everything.

They have read the books. They have watched the documentaries. Many of them served. Many worked in aviation, naval, or defence industries.

**If a fact is wrong, they will find it. And they will comment.**

One factual error does not just damage one video. It damages the channel's authority.

The rule is simple: **be precise or be silent.**

If a fact cannot be verified to reasonable confidence — soften the claim or remove it. A hedged truth is always better than a confident error.

---

## STEP 1: TOPIC VALIDATION

Before beginning any research, verify that the topic can support a complete documentary.

A good topic must naturally produce:
- A clear one-sentence thesis
- At least 15 strong units with distinct stories
- A meaningful narrative arc with escalation or contradiction
- Engineering lessons that are not repetitive across units

**If the topic cannot satisfy these conditions — reject it or narrow it before writing.**

A topic that sounds broad enough ("Every US Fighter Ever Built") may still fail validation if most units would produce identical engineering paragraphs. A narrower topic with stronger stories is always preferable to a broad topic with thin ones.

---

## STEP 2: UNIT SELECTION

### Video length rules

| Units | Result |
|-------|--------|
| Below 15 | Too short — expand topic scope or merge with related topic |
| 15–23 | Acceptable for narrow topics with deep individual stories |
| 24–30 | Optimal for standard DvsU format |
| 31–32 | Maximum — only if every unit is genuinely necessary |
| Above 32 | Split into Part 1 / Part 2 before writing |

### Never force the unit count

The target is 24–30 units, but only if 24–30 strong units genuinely exist.

Never invent weaker paragraphs simply to reach the target count.

If only 18–20 units meet the editorial standard, prefer a shorter documentary over lowering the quality threshold.

**Quality always determines quantity — never the other way around.**

### Process: Longlist → Shortlist → Rejected list

**Longlist:** All potentially relevant unit candidates for this topic.

**Shortlist:** 24–30 units selected by story strength.

**Part 1 / Part 2 rule:** Split only when enough strong units remain to support another complete documentary. Do not create Part 2 simply because additional machines exist. Part 2 must have its own thesis, narrative arc, and engineering argument — not just the overflow from Part 1.

**Rejected list:** What was excluded and why. This prevents AI from re-inserting excluded units during writing.

### Story strength ranking

When two units satisfy the same engineering role in the video, prefer the one with the stronger story.

**A machine should never be selected because it is famous.**
**It should be selected because it creates a stronger paragraph.**

Priority order for unit selection:
1. Strong story (unique conflict, failure, paradox, unexpected outcome)
2. Strong engineering lesson (decision that changed doctrine or design)
3. Strong visual identity (unmistakable image, distinct from adjacent units)
4. Historical importance
5. Fame

**Fame alone is never sufficient for inclusion.**

Obscure machines with exceptional stories are often stronger than famous machines with predictable stories.

### 5-criteria evaluation for each unit

Every unit candidate is evaluated on:

1. **Unique story** — Does this unit have its own story, not just the fact that it existed?
2. **Engineering decision** — Does it illustrate a meaningful design, procurement, or doctrinal decision?
3. **Conflict / failure / controversy** — Is there tension? A trade-off? A mistake? An unexpected outcome?
4. **Visual distinction** — Can one unmistakable image be generated for it, distinct from adjacent units?
5. **Research confidence** — Are there enough verified sources to write with confidence?

**If a unit does not pass at least 3 of 5 criteria — exclude it.**

### Repetition check

Before finalising the shortlist, compare every unit against every other unit.

**If two units would tell essentially the same engineering story — remove one.**

Every paragraph should answer a different historical question. No two paragraphs in the same video should produce the same thesis.

### Balance the documentary

After forming the shortlist, verify the overall balance before writing.

Avoid clustering too many similar units together. Mix:
- Successes and failures
- Famous and obscure machines
- Prototypes and production vehicles
- Operational stories and engineering decisions
- Combat use and procurement failures

Variety improves retention. Even if five consecutive units are individually strong, five consecutive units of the same type will flatten the documentary's rhythm.

### What makes a unit strong for DvsU

Include machines that:
- Were hated by the people who served on them
- Solved a problem that didn't exist
- Were right but arrived too late
- Were copied by the enemy
- Were cancelled weeks before the war they were built for
- Created a doctrinal lesson through failure
- Represent a trade-off where the wrong choice was made — and why

Exclude machines that:
- Simply existed and performed their role without a distinct story
- Are interesting only because many were built
- Are variants of an already-included unit with no new engineering idea
- Cannot be visually distinguished from an adjacent unit
- Would produce a paragraph with the same thesis as another unit already in the video

### Minimum research depth per unit

Before a unit is confirmed on the shortlist, verify:

1. Verified identity (correct name, designation, classification)
2. Verified dates and service period
3. One clear engineering reason for inclusion
4. One memorable fact most viewers are unlikely to know
5. Source confidence level (Tier 1–3 as defined below)

**If any of these five are missing — replace the unit.**

### The final unit test

Before approving any unit, ask:

**"If this paragraph disappeared, would the documentary become weaker?"**

If the answer is no — replace the unit.

Second check: **"If removing this unit changes nothing except the unit count — it should not be in the documentary."**

---

## STEP 3: PRIMARY SOURCE CHECK

For every unit on the shortlist, identify at minimum:
- One primary or authoritative secondary source confirming: what it was, when it served, what it did, what made it significant
- One specific verifiable fact that is not common knowledge
- One human detail — pilot memoir, crew account, official inquiry, combat report — if available

---

## STEP 4: CROSS-REFERENCE DISPUTED CLAIMS

Any claim involving:
- Exact production numbers
- Kill ratios or loss statistics
- "First ever" / "only ever" / "most" superlatives
- Specifications (speed, range, payload, armour thickness)
- Dates of specific events

...must be confirmed in at least **two independent sources** before appearing as a stated fact.

If two sources disagree — use the more conservative number and hedge: *"approximately," "around," "estimated at."*

---

## STEP 5: SUPERLATIVE AUDIT

Before finalising the script, find every instance of:
"first ever" / "only" / "most" / "largest" / "fastest" / "never before" / "no other"

For each one: can it be verified? If not — soften or remove.

*"One of the first"* is almost always safer than *"the first."*
*"Among the most"* is almost always safer than *"the most."*

---

## STEP 6: FINAL READ FOR ANACHRONISMS

Check that no terminology, concept, or technology is described using language that didn't exist in the period being discussed.

---

## CONTROVERSY IS NOT CLICKBAIT

DvsU is built on genuine historical controversy:
- Disputed doctrine
- Failed procurement
- Engineering trade-offs
- Conflicting military priorities
- Decisions that seemed right at the time and proved catastrophically wrong

**Never manufacture controversy. Never exaggerate disagreement where historians broadly agree.**

If the controversy is real — it needs no amplification.
If the controversy has to be invented — the unit is probably wrong for this video.

---

## SUFFICIENT RESEARCH

Do not continue collecting sources indefinitely.

Once the engineering thesis, key facts, human detail, and verification requirements are satisfied for every unit — begin writing.

**Research exists to support the story, not replace it.**

If research continues beyond what is needed to write with confidence, stop.

---

## TRUSTED SOURCE HIERARCHY

### Tier 1 — Primary sources (highest authority)
- Official military documents, after-action reports, procurement records
- Manufacturer technical manuals and specifications
- Congressional testimony and government inquiries
- Published memoirs by named participants (pilots, crew, commanders)
- Official investigation reports (accident inquiries, board of inquiry findings)

### Tier 2 — Authoritative secondary sources
- Peer-reviewed military history journals
- Books by Naval Institute Press, Osprey Publishing, Jane's, Smithsonian Institution Press
- Official service histories published by national military archives
- Museum documentation (NASM, Imperial War Museum, Tank Museum Bovington)

### Tier 3 — Reliable reference sources
- Jane's All the World's Aircraft / Fighting Ships / Armour and Artillery
- Encyclopedia of military aircraft / ships / vehicles from established publishers
- National archive databases (NARA, The National Archives UK, Bundesarchiv)

### Tier 4 — Use with caution, always cross-reference
- Wikipedia (useful for orientation and finding source leads — never cite as sole source)
- General aviation/military history websites
- YouTube documentaries (can lead to primary sources, not a source itself)

### Never use as source
- Anonymous forum posts
- Social media
- Unattributed statistics
- "Common knowledge" without a verifiable origin

---

## THE HUMAN DETAIL RULE

**Whenever possible, include one documented human perspective within the first three paragraphs of the video.**

This is one of the most powerful retention tools in the DvsU format. It immediately establishes that the channel deals in real history, not just specifications.

**If a verified human account exists for one of the first three units — use it.**
**If no verified human account exists — do not invent one.**

Use instead: an official inquiry finding, a named engineer's documented statement, or a named commander's recorded decision.

**Human details should illuminate the engineering decision, not replace it.**
The anecdote supports the thesis. It never becomes the thesis.

Human detail is preferred early because it creates trust. But accuracy is always more important than emotion.

### What counts as a human detail

- A direct quote from a pilot's memoir about flying this aircraft
- A crew member's account of what it was like to serve on this ship or tank
- A named engineer's or designer's documented statement about the design decision
- An official inquiry finding that includes crew testimony
- A documented moment from a specific named person's experience

### How to source human details

**Pilots and aircrew:** Published memoirs, IWM Sound Archive, US Library of Congress Veterans History Project, unit histories

**Naval crew:** Ship's logs and war diaries (national archives), published memoirs, Naval Institute Proceedings oral histories

**Tank crew:** Regimental histories, published memoirs, Tank Museum Bovington oral history collection

**Engineers and designers:** Corporate histories, published interviews in aviation/engineering press, declassified letters and internal memos

### Format for human details in the script

The human detail must be:
- Attributed to a named person ("Colonel Richard Bong described" — not "a pilot said")
- Brief enough to fit within the 95–120 word paragraph
- Specific enough to be verifiable
- Directly connected to the paragraph's engineering thesis

---

## THE SURPRISING FACT REQUIREMENT

**Every unit must contain one fact that is surprising even to military history enthusiasts.**

If the paragraph contains only facts commonly found in the first paragraph of a Wikipedia article — the research is not deep enough.

Go further. Find the number that shocked the procurement committee. Find the test result that contradicted the specification. Find the operational discovery that changed the doctrine.

---

## NUMBERS AND STATISTICS: RULES

### Write numbers as words for voiceover
"twelve thousand, seven hundred and thirty-one" not "12,731"
Exception: model designations (B-52, MiG-21, F-86)

### Approximate when precision is uncertain
Use: *"approximately," "around," "roughly," "estimated at," "between X and Y"*

### Production numbers — always hedge unless sourced
### Kill ratios — always hedge ("the claimed kill ratio was...")
### Specifications — use official sources where possible; note if operational reality differed

---

## WHAT TO DO WHEN SOURCES CONFLICT

1. Use the more conservative figure
2. Add a hedge
3. If the conflict is historically significant — mention it: *"The exact number remains disputed..."*
4. Never resolve a genuine historical dispute by picking the higher or more dramatic number

---

## HIGH-RISK CLAIM CATEGORIES

**"First ever" claims** — Use *"among the first"* unless primacy is definitively established.

**Cold War statistics** — Politically influenced documents. Hedge appropriately.

**Soviet/Russian production numbers** — Cross-reference with post-1991 scholarship.

**WWII aerial kill claims** — Use confirmed kills from official records, not claimed figures.

**"Last surviving" claims** — Use *"one of the few surviving"* rather than *"the only surviving."*

---

## EDITORIAL PRIORITY

When two editorial decisions conflict, resolve them in this order:

1. **Accuracy** — never compromised
2. **Story quality** — the strength of the individual paragraph
3. **Engineering insight** — the lesson the unit contributes to the thesis
4. **Narrative flow** — how the unit connects to adjacent units
5. **Completeness** — whether the topic feels fully covered

Never sacrifice a higher priority to improve a lower one.

## FINAL VERIFICATION CHECKLIST

- [ ] Topic validation passed (thesis, 15+ units, arc, non-repetitive lessons)
- [ ] Longlist created, shortlist selected (24–30), rejected list documented
- [ ] Every unit passes minimum 3 of 5 selection criteria
- [ ] Story strength ranking applied — fame alone did not select any unit
- [ ] Repetition check completed — no two units tell the same engineering story
- [ ] Every unit passes the final unit test: "Would the documentary be weaker without it?"
- [ ] Every unit has: verified identity, dates, engineering reason, surprising fact, source confidence
- [ ] Every production number hedged or sourced
- [ ] Every superlative verified or softened
- [ ] Every human detail attributed to a named person and connected to the engineering thesis
- [ ] No orphan facts
- [ ] All disputed statistics cross-referenced
- [ ] Controversy is genuine, not manufactured
- [ ] Research stopped at sufficient depth — not continued indefinitely
- [ ] Final question: would a retired naval officer, military historian, or veteran aviation enthusiast find anything to correct?

**If the answer to the last question is "probably not" — the script is ready.**
