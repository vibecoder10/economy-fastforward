## Scene 1 (137 words)

The United States Navy entered the carrier age without building a carrier, converting the collier USS Jupiter to test whether aircraft could operate effectively from a moving deck at sea. Recommissioned as USS Langley on March 20, 1922, the converted hull retained its original turbo-electric propulsion from coal-hauling service and gained a flush flight deck over the cargo holds. The Washington Naval Treaty exempted experimental vessels from tonnage limits, allowing Langley to proceed as a proof-of-concept platform rather than a frontline warship. Langley served as America's first flattop before sailing into Mare Island Navy Yard in October 1936 for conversion to a seaplane tender, then met Japanese bombers in February 1942 and was scuttled by her own escorts. The ship that taught the Navy how to launch and recover aircraft spent the war as a seaplane tender.

## Scene 2 (135 words)

Ordered around 1916 as a battlecruiser, the hull that would become CV-2 USS Lexington entered service on December 14, 1927, after the Washington Naval Treaty forced its conversion into one of the Navy's first aircraft carriers. The battlecruiser origins delivered massive displacement and speed, but the carrier conversion retained volatile gasoline fuel systems below decks that would prove catastrophic under combat stress. During battle around 1942, a tremendous explosion caused by ignited gasoline vapors shook Lexington after initial torpedo damage, and fire raged beyond all control despite the crew's efforts. Destroyer USS Phelps fired two torpedoes into the CV-2 hull to hasten the end, and with one last heavy explosion, USS Lexington sank at about 1956 on the eighth. The treaty conversion created a fleet carrier, but the battlecruiser fuel architecture destroyed it from within.

## Scene 3 (141 words)

The Washington Naval Treaty forced the Navy to convert Battle Cruiser Three into an aircraft carrier, reclassified as CV-3 and commissioned in November of about 1927 as USS Saratoga. The day after Pearl Harbor, she hurriedly departed carrying Marine aircraft to reinforce Wake Island, fitted with two hydraulically powered centerline elevators to handle the strike planes her battle cruiser speed was meant to protect. The Saratoga force was delayed by the low speed of its oiler and the necessity to refuel destroyers. During peacetime fleet problems, she had proven the concept by detaching with a single cruiser escort to simulate a strike on the Panama Canal, and in roughly one January experiment, the rigid airship Los Angeles moored to her stern to take on fuel and stores. The Navy had built a fast carrier before it understood how to use one.

## Scene 4 (116 words)

Laid down in September 1931, CV-4 USS Ranger became the first warship designed as an aircraft carrier from the keel up rather than converted from a battle cruiser hull. The Navy initially planned her as a flush-decker with nothing extending above the flight deck, a radical departure from the towering island structures of Lexington and Saratoga. To meet treaty tonnage limits, Ranger was commissioned around 1934 without torpedo stowage or a torpedo bomber squadron. She led a task force of four escort carriers during the amphibious invasion of French Morocco on the morning of November eighth, 1942, providing air superiority for the first major offensive in the Atlantic theater. The treaty experiment became a combat workhorse.

## Scene 5 (150 words)

Yorktown was the first American carrier whose design drew from actual fleet experience with both the converted battlecruisers of the Lexington class and the primitive experimental Langley. Commissioned at Norfolk on September 30, 1937, she operated along the eastern seaboard and Caribbean through 1939, then shifted to Pacific waters until April 1941 when German U-boat success against British shipping pulled American naval strength back to the Atlantic. After Pearl Harbor she returned to the Pacific, escorting a Marine convoy to Samoa in January 1942. Yorktown spent one hundred days away from a major port during her early combat deployment, then was lost at Midway on June seventh, about 1942 and struck from the register that October, and two months later the Essex-class hull numbered CV-10 had her name changed from Bon Homme Richard to Yorktown. The first purpose-built fleet carrier died proving the operational lessons that had shaped her design.

## Scene 6 (135 words)

Enterprise was completing a transport mission, returning to Hawaii after moving Marine Fighter Squadron about 211 to Wake Island on December seventh, 1941, missing Pearl Harbor by hours and entering the war as one of three operational Pacific carriers. The Yorktown-class design displaced nineteen thousand eight hundred tons, stretched over eight hundred nine feet, and carried eighty aircraft at thirty-three knots with a crew of nearly three thousand. A postwar campaign led by Fleet Admiral Halsey failed to raise funds for museum preservation despite her unmatched combat record. Enterprise fought in more major actions than any other United States ship, earned twenty battle stars and a Presidential Unit Citation, then was decommissioned in February about 1947 and scrapped by 1960. The war's most decorated carrier became razor blades while lesser ships found immortality as museums.

## Scene 7 (145 words)

The Navy had treaty tonnage left over after building the Yorktown class, just enough for one more carrier but not enough to build it properly. CV-7 USS Wasp, laid down in April 1936 and launched in April 1939, became a reduced-size Yorktown with minimal armor protection, weak torpedo defenses, and the Navy's first deck-edge elevator to save internal space. The design flaws were recognized during construction but could not be remedied within the allowed displacement, leaving Wasp more vulnerable than any other carrier entering the war. After twice ferrying British fighters to Malta in April 1942, she transferred to the Pacific in June to replace the Coral Sea and Midway losses, serving as launching platform for future ace David McCampbell until enemy torpedoes exploited her weak underwater protection. Treaty math built a carrier the Navy knew couldn't survive the war it was about to fight.

## Scene 8 (126 words)

Washington Naval Treaty tonnage caps forced the United States to allocate remaining carrier displacement to a smaller design after two Yorktowns, but the Navy instead laid down a third full-size fleet carrier in September 1939. Newport News delivered CV-8 with a waterline length of seven hundred seventy feet, eight five-inch dual-purpose guns, and radar installed atop her tripod mast by June about 1942. Commissioned in October 1941, Hornet steamed with battleships Washington and North Carolina for shakedown training on December twenty-seventh as war opened around her. Abandoned after irreparable bomb and torpedo damage, she became the last full-size American carrier lost to enemy action in World War Two when Japanese destroyers sank her around late October 1942. The treaty's final loophole bought eleven months of war.

## Scene 9 (156 words)

Unencumbered by the latest succession of pre-war naval treaty limits that had constrained Yorktown-class carriers, the Essex design stretched over sixty feet longer, nearly ten feet wider, and more than a third heavier, trading the compromises of arms control for enhanced anti-aircraft defense and armor protection. Essex could carry roughly one hundred fighter aircraft, moved rapidly from hangar to flight deck by an outboard elevator, while improved survivability systems addressed the combat lessons treaty designers had been forced to defer. The investment in protection paid immediate dividends: despite several vessels sustaining very heavy damage in combat, no Essex-class ship was lost to enemy action. Twenty-four vessels in both short-hull and long-hull versions formed the backbone of the Navy from mid-1943, then continued as the heart of carrier operations through successive modernization programs into around the early 1960s, until replaced by supercarriers. Treaty freedom bought a design durable enough to span the propeller and jet ages both.

## Scene 10 (140 words)

Launched in March 1945 and commissioned that September, Midway arrived as an armored wartime design delivered into peacetime. Built with an armored flight deck and a roughly one hundred twenty plane air group, the carrier embodied lessons learned from kamikaze strikes, scaled to handle the heaviest aircraft the Navy could envision. That ambition came at a cost: Midway became the first carrier too large to transit the Panama Canal, forcing operational planning around deployment constraints. Redesignated an attack carrier in October 1952, Midway proved its value not in the war it missed but as the testbed where Naval Air Test Center pilots demonstrated the angled deck concept over several days in May 1952, validating the geometry that would define jet operations for every supercarrier to follow. The ship built for propeller warfare became the laboratory that solved the jet age.

## Scene 11 (142 words)

CVB-42 was laid down in December about 1943 as a forty-five-thousand-ton Midway-class carrier, nine hundred sixty-eight feet long with a beam of one hundred thirteen feet and designed to operate one hundred thirty-seven aircraft at thirty-three knots. Originally named Coral Sea, the hull was renamed Franklin D Roosevelt on May eighth about 1945 following the president's death, then commissioned that October with eighteen five-inch guns and eighty-four forty-millimeter mounts. The straight-deck configuration and armament were already dated by commissioning, requiring a prolonged overhaul at Norfolk Naval Shipyard starting July about 1947 to modernize equipment and facilities. Roosevelt was manufactured to transport and deploy aircraft through sea during the war but served roughly thirty years through two wars and seven presidencies before transfer to Kearney Shipyards for scrapping in April 1978. A wartime design outlasted the conflict it was built for by decades.

## Scene 12 (132 words)

The Navy commissioned CVB-43 in October 1947 to prove that carrier-based atomic bomb attacks could work, sending her to the Mediterranean the following May with the Sixth Fleet after Caribbean training runs validated the concept. Newport News Shipbuilding delivered the hull with an armored flight deck and enough displacement to handle the weight and blast radius of nuclear ordnance. An escort carrier had already carried the name Coral Sea, forcing the Navy to rename that ship Anzio so the new atomic-capable flattop could honor the early carrier battle fought entirely by aircraft beyond visual range. The ship was decommissioned for conversion in May about 1957 and recommissioned in January 1960, then served another three decades as a conventional strike platform. Built to deliver the bomb, she spent her career launching everything else.

## Scene 13 (166 words)

Commissioned in October 1955 as the early completed supercarrier, Forrestal represented the Cold War answer to Soviet naval expansion and the demand for a mobile airfield capable of projecting nuclear strike power across oceans. The design delivered a flight deck spanning over four acres to accommodate more than eighty combat aircraft, a scale that dwarfed every carrier before it and established the template for every supercarrier that followed. That unprecedented size and aircraft density, however, created fuel and ordnance concentrations that turned minor accidents into catastrophic events, a vulnerability the design had not anticipated. After confronting Libya around 1981 and receiving a service life extension from 1983 to 1985, Forrestal was redesignated from attack carrier CVA 59 to multi-purpose CV 59 in June 1975, decommissioned in the 1990s, stripped as a parts hulk for active carriers, and finally sold for one cent to a Brownsville scrapyard in October 2013. A 1967 blaze killed one hundred thirty-four sailors and eclipsed every operational success the fleet had expected.

## Scene 14 (128 words)

Laid down in December 1952 as the second supercarrier hull, CV-60 USS Saratoga entered a Navy still organizing carriers by strike specialization, each ship optimized for a single combat role. The carrier operated an air wing of roughly twenty-seven hundred personnel, defended by three NATO Sea Sparrow launchers and three Phalanx close-in weapon systems. That single-mission design proved limiting: after an eleven-month modernization overhaul beginning in January about 1968, Saratoga still faced a Navy rethinking the economics of role-dedicated flattops. On June thirtieth, about 1972, the ship was redesignated from attack aircraft carrier CVA 60 to multi-purpose aircraft carrier CV 60, acknowledging that fleet reality had outgrown the original strike-doctrine. The hull served until 1994, then sold for one cent and scrapped—a supercarrier that outlasted its own category.

## Scene 15 (145 words)

Ranger's elder sisters Forrestal and Saratoga had been laid down as axial-deck ships and converted for an angled deck while under construction, but Ranger was laid down in August 1954 as the first of the class designed from the keel for the angled configuration. The design allowed the carrier to launch and recover aircraft simultaneously, a capability demonstrated when Ranger deployed near French Polynesia to monitor nuclear tests on Mururoa Atoll by launching and recovering a Lockheed U-2 from its flight deck. Museum plans for Ranger were canceled after roughly thirty-six years of service. Commissioned as attack aircraft carrier CVA 61, Ranger was redesignated as multi-purpose aircraft carrier CV 61 on June thirtieth 1975, then launched airstrikes against Iraqi targets during Operation Desert Storm in 1991 before decommissioning in 1993 and sale for scrapping in 2014. The mission had changed, and Ranger changed with it.

## Scene 16 (130 words)

Brooklyn Navy Yard won the contract for Independence, the fourth Forrestal-class carrier, on July second, 1954, and launched her four years later into a fleet that expected decades of jet operations from angled decks. Like her sisters, Independence carried an angled flight deck, long-range search radar, height-finding radar, and defensive armament designed to operate a variety of jet aircraft. By around 1998, her material condition had deteriorated so badly that the Navy refused to retain her as a museum. Commissioned as attack aircraft carrier CVA-62, she was redesignated multi-purpose carrier CV-62 on February 28, about 1973, served thirty-nine years of active duty, and became the last of the four Forrestals scrapped when her dismantling finished in December 2018. Brooklyn built a workhorse that outlasted her design but not her steel.

## Scene 17 (153 words)

The New York Shipbuilding Corporation laid down Kitty Hawk's keel in December 1956, but her mass ruled out the conventional slide-down launch method because engineers feared she might hit the Philadelphia shore across the Delaware River. Instead, the yard launched her by flooding the drydock in May 1960, a solution that reflected both her scale and the river's narrow geometry. Commissioned as attack aircraft carrier CVA-63 in April 1961, she was redesignated multi-purpose carrier CV-63 twelve years later as the Navy's mission priorities shifted. The lead ship of her class served for ten years as the forward-deployed carrier at Yokosuka, Japan, and was the last of the three Kitty Hawk-class carriers to decommission in May two thousand nine before leaving Puget Sound under tow for scrapping in Brownsville, Texas, in January about 2022. Built too large to launch conventionally, she served long enough to outlive her original attack mission and every museum proposal.

## Scene 18 (139 words)

The Navy awarded New York Naval Shipyard the contract to build its second Kitty Hawk-class carrier on about July 1, 1956, laying her keel fourteen months later as the third ship to bear the name Constellation. Christened in October 1960, the conventionally powered flattop was designed to operate the same air wing as her predecessor, delivering roughly the same carrier capability to stations worldwide. A catastrophic fire tore through the incomplete hull on December 19, 1960, killing fifty workers and delaying commissioning nearly a year while the Navy rebuilt damaged compartments. Delivered as attack aircraft carrier CVA about 64 in October 1961, she was redesignated multi-purpose carrier CV 64 in June 1975 and served until decommissioning in August 2003 after forty-one years of naval service. The fire that nearly destroyed her became a footnote to four decades at sea.

## Scene 19 (125 words)

When Newport News Shipbuilding laid the keel in February 1958, the Navy committed to proving that a nuclear-powered aircraft carrier could replace the oil-burning fleet. Enterprise stretched eleven hundred twenty-three feet long and two hundred fifty-seven feet wide, with roughly four and a half acres of flight deck for nearly one hundred fighter planes. The eight reactors bought unlimited range but demanded constant refurbishment, and by about 2022 no American shipyard possessed the capacity to dismantle the radioactive hull. Enterprise served virtually every major American military action since commissioning, and on February 20th, about 1962, acted as a tracking and measuring station for the flight of Friendship Seven. The prototype that proved nuclear carriers could work became the ship the Navy could not retire cleanly.

## Scene 20 (141 words)

Commissioned at Norfolk Naval Shipyard on about 23 January 1965, CV-66 USS America entered service as carrier presence was demanded from the Mediterranean to Vietnam. Roughly one of the large conventionally powered aircraft carriers ever built, America belonged to the Kitty Hawk class, designed to project airpower without nuclear propulsion. Despite decades of deployments—Vietnam, Lebanon, the Persian Gulf, and Desert Storm—the ship was denied museum status and scuttled southeast of Cape Hatteras in 2005 after four weeks of damage tests, over the protests of former crew members. America had fought in Vietnam, deterred conflict during the Six Day War around 1967, and served through both Gulf conflicts before becoming the first large carrier since Operation Crossroads in 1946 to be deliberately destroyed in weapons trials. The service chose data over memory, turning a workhorse into the blueprint for killing its successors.

## Scene 21 (135 words)

Laid down in October 1964 at Newport News Shipbuilding, Kennedy entered service as the Navy's final conventionally-powered supercarrier, commissioned in September 1968 when nuclear propulsion had already become the fleet standard. The last carrier built without a nuclear reactor, Kennedy operated as a full-deck attack platform capable of launching strike aircraft across the Atlantic. Transferred to the Naval Reserve Force around 1995, the ship faced an uncertain future as budget pressures and the nuclear carrier transition threatened to end her operational life early. Initially commissioned as attack aircraft carrier CVA sixty-seven, she was redesignated multi-purpose carrier CV sixty-seven in December 1974, then returned to the active fleet in October 2000 before final decommissioning in August 2007. The Navy's last conventional carrier served decades of frontline operations, then was sold for one cent to a scrapyard.

## Scene 23 (138 words)
