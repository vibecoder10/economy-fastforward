Every U.S. Military Helicopter Ever Built
DvsU — Production Script  |  30 Units  |  ~22 min runtime
The first military helicopter to enter mass production was not built for combat. It was built to rescue downed pilots, and it worked so well that every search-and-rescue helicopter for the next eighty years traces its mission to it. On April 25th, 1944, in Burma, Lieutenant Carter Harman flew an R-4 behind Japanese lines and pulled a downed crew member from a jungle clearing — the first helicopter rescue in combat history. Around one hundred and thirty were built. It did not win a battle. It defined what helicopters were for.
No military helicopter became more recognizable than the Bell H-13 Sioux — but the television show that made its bubble canopy famous captured almost nothing the real aircraft accomplished. In Korea, H-13s flew more than eighteen thousand medical evacuation missions, getting wounded men to surgery in minutes rather than hours and producing measurably better survival outcomes than any previous conflict had recorded. More than two thousand five hundred were produced. The TV show got the ratings. Korea got the result.
Recognizing the need for a helicopter that could carry soldiers rather than observe them, the U.S. Army turned to the Sikorsky H-19 Chickasaw in 1950 and made a decision that changed land warfare permanently. In Korea, the H-19 moved the first complete American combat unit under fire by air — not as a test, but as a deliberate tactical choice. Around twelve hundred and eighty were built and exported to thirty countries. Every other aircraft on this list is measured against established doctrine. The H-19 is the point where the doctrine began.
Every fixed-wing pilot who saw the Piasecki H-21 Shawnee called it the Flying Banana and meant it as an insult. On December 11th, 1961, thirty-three of them lifted a thousand South Vietnamese troops in Operation Chopper — the first large-scale helicopter combat assault in American military history — proving the concept of air mobility before a single doctrine existed to govern it. The H-21 was replaced by the Huey before the war became large enough to be remembered by popular history. The Huey got the war. The Shawnee invented it.
Where conventional design produced conventional results, the Kaman H-43 Huskie took a different path. Its intermeshing counter-rotating rotors gave it unmatched hover stability directly over a fire, and around two hundred and sixty served as the primary crash rescue platform at U.S. Air Force forward bases in Vietnam, flying toward burning aircraft while crews inside were still alive. It held the Air Force record for lives saved per aircraft type for over forty years. It flew toward the crashes. That was the whole job.
The Army ordered the Bell UH-1 Iroquois as a medical evacuation platform and specifically stated it was not to be used as a gunship. Within three years it had become the most heavily armed rotorcraft in the world. By 1966 a single Huey could carry rockets, machine guns, and a minigun on an airframe built to carry stretchers. More than sixteen thousand were built — more than any military helicopter in history. Its two-blade rotor became the defining sound of the Vietnam War. They ordered an ambulance. They got an air force.
As the war in Vietnam demanded heavier airlift, Boeing Vertol delivered the CH-47 Chinook in 1962 and kept delivering it for the next six decades — making it among the longest continuously manufactured military helicopters in aviation history. The current CH-47F carries around sixty-eight percent more payload than the 1962 original. More than twelve hundred have been built and delivered to sixteen countries. Every decade the Army commissions a study on replacing it. Every decade the Chinook keeps flying.
The outbreak of combat losses among troops waiting for a purpose-built gunship brought Bell a contract in September 1965 with a single demand: deliver a combat-ready helicopter in under two years. The AH-1 Cobra arrived in Vietnam in 1967, twenty-three months after signing — the fastest combat aircraft development in U.S. history. The Marines still fly a direct descendant, the AH-1Z Viper, in 2024, fifty-seven years after the original entered service. The Army needed it yesterday. Bell delivered it in twenty-three months.
No American helicopter looked less finished than the Sikorsky CH-54 Tarhe, and no American helicopter recovered more from the battlefield. The Skycrane had no fuselage and almost nothing between the pilots and the ground, because removing that structure was precisely the point: it created the clearance to sling-load what nothing else could reach. Around one hundred and five were built, and in Vietnam they recovered more downed aircraft than all other platforms combined. It looked like half a helicopter. It did the work of three.
The OH-6 Cayuse's entire combat role was built around one brutal calculation: fly into enemy fire to draw a reaction. The tactic had an official name — the Pink Team — and a fixed logic: the Loach flew low and slow until someone revealed their position, and an AH-1 orbiting above destroyed what had just announced itself. The OH-6 recorded the highest crew decoration rate per airframe of any helicopter in Vietnam as a direct result. Around fourteen hundred were built. The machine was designed to be bait. The pilots volunteered anyway.
Where others hesitated, the Sikorsky HH-3E Jolly Green Giant flew into North Vietnam knowing the enemy had positioned radar-guided anti-aircraft systems specifically to shoot down rescue helicopters. The HH-3E was the first helicopter equipped with in-flight refueling, extending its reach deep into denied airspace. Around forty-two were lost in combat over Southeast Asia. The Air Force had decided no downed airman would be left behind regardless of what that cost the rescuers. The enemy knew when the rescue was coming. The Jolly Green came anyway.
Built for the Marines and flown in the final hours of the Vietnam War to evacuate Saigon, the Sikorsky CH-53 Sea Stallion produced the most reproduced photograph of American defeat — and in doing so buried its own operational record. Operation Frequent Wind, April 29th and 30th, 1975: CH-53s moved more than two thousand five hundred people in eighteen hours. Three weeks earlier the same type had carried the assault force in the Mayaguez rescue. History remembered the rooftop. The Marines remembered the mission.
Among the first helicopters purpose-designed for anti-submarine warfare, the Sikorsky SH-3 Sea King used dipping sonar in open-ocean operations and recovered astronauts after every Apollo mission. It also became the helicopter that carried the President — a role it was never designed for. Around seven hundred and ninety were built. The SH-3H variant tracked Soviet submarines in the Atlantic for thirty-five years, a Cold War record almost entirely absent from popular history. It hunted submarines for thirty-five years. It is remembered for carrying presidents.
The Boeing Vertol CH-46 Sea Knight entered Marine service in 1964 with a replacement already being discussed. That replacement arrived in 2015, fifty-one years later, after the CH-46 had flown through Vietnam, Lebanon, the Gulf War, and two decades of operations in Iraq and Afghanistan. No successor program across five consecutive decades could match its amphibious assault performance at acceptable cost. The MV-22 Osprey finally ended the run. Five decades of replacement programs. Five decades of still flying.
The loss rates of Vietnam-era helicopters in crashes produced one lesson the Army encoded directly into its next design requirement: the aircraft must survive the impact. The Sikorsky UH-60 Black Hawk was engineered to withstand a twenty-gravity crash, and the survivable airframe standard it introduced has since been adopted across every subsequent U.S. rotorcraft program. More than four thousand have been built. Twenty-eight countries operate it. The breakthrough was not the speed or the payload. They designed it to crash safely. Nobody talks about that part.
Congress cancelled the AH-64 Apache program three times. The Army cancelled it twice. The Government Accountability Office called it the most expensive helicopter development effort in U.S. history. On January 17th, 1991, around two hundred and seventy-seven Apaches opened Desert Storm and destroyed more than five hundred Iraqi armored vehicles in the first twenty-four hours — a result no ground force could have matched in the same window. The program is now in its fifth production decade. They cancelled it five times. Iraq changed the math.
No helicopter that entered service carried more technology simultaneously than the Sikorsky MH-53J Pave Low: terrain-following radar, forward-looking infrared, inertial navigation, and GPS integration — all classified for most of its operational life. On January 17th, 1991, MH-53Js led the Gulf War's opening strike, guiding Army Apaches to Iraqi radar stations using GPS coordinates in one of the first GPS-guided strike missions in military history. Every sensor system it pioneered is now standard across U.S. special operations aviation. Most people have never heard of it.
Where a purpose-built replacement would have been ideal, the Kaman SH-2 Seasprite was converted from a utility transport into a frontline anti-submarine platform because the Navy had no better option — and it remained in that role for thirty-eight U.S. service years because every replacement program was cancelled. New Zealand flew updated variants until 2016, giving the type fifty-four total years of service for an airframe designed to move cargo between ships. It was never meant to hunt submarines. It spent forty years doing it.
The mission at Takur Ghar on March 4th, 2002 began when a Navy SEAL fell onto an enemy-held mountain at ten thousand feet. An MH-47 flew in, took an RPG hit, lost a crew member, and crash-landed. The surviving crew fought on foot while a second aircraft came under fire. Seven Americans died on that mountain. The first MH-47 crew knew the full situation when they turned back for a second approach. They went back for one man. They fought their way to him on foot.
Thirty people died in crashes during its development. Congress attempted to cancel it eleven times. Dick Cheney terminated the Bell-Boeing V-22 Osprey twice as Secretary of Defense. It has since accumulated more than six hundred thousand operational flight hours and remains the only aircraft in U.S. service that cannot be replaced by either a helicopter or a fixed-wing plane. Operational capability came in 2007. The V-22 at maturity is a profoundly different machine from the one in the development crash sequence. They said it was unflyable. It turned out to be irreplaceable.
No single mission revealed more about what special operations aviation had become than the raid on Osama bin Laden's compound on May 2nd, 2011. A heavily modified, radar-absorbing Black Hawk variant flew the operation — and when one crashed in the courtyard, the crew destroyed it so completely that Pakistani and reportedly Chinese analysts spent months examining the tail section without reconstructing what they were looking at. The specific modifications remain classified. The existence of the aircraft is confirmed. Its specifications are not. It flew one mission. It is still classified.
Pararescuemen attached to the Sikorsky HH-60 Pave Hawk operate under a motto no other U.S. aviation specialty uses: That Others May Live. The Pave Hawk has deployed in every American conflict since Grenada, flying into active combat zones after every other recovery option has failed. Around one hundred and twelve were built. Its pararescuemen hold the highest per-capita decoration rate of any Air Force specialty. No airframe specification captures that commitment. The motto is not marketing. It is the mission statement.
The Boeing A/MH-6 Little Bird weighs around two thousand eight hundred pounds, carries six operators on external benches bolted to the skids, and flies them at nearly one hundred and seventy miles per hour fully exposed to the slipstream. It fits on ship decks, rooftops, and urban corridors nothing larger can reach. Its operational record spans Grenada, Panama, Somalia, Iraq, and Afghanistan — most of those missions in files that will not appear in any public record. Small enough to fit anywhere. Too classified to count.
Where the Marine Corps needed raw lift, the Sikorsky CH-53E Super Stallion provided it: three engines, a seven-blade rotor, and around thirty-six thousand pounds of external capacity that could move artillery and disabled armor where no other Western helicopter could follow. A replacement has been in development since 2000. The CH-53K King Stallion entered limited service in 2022. Full transition is not expected until the 2030s. More than one hundred and fifty CH-53Es remain in operation. They have been waiting for the replacement for twenty-five years. It has not stopped flying.
The Army spent more than six billion dollars over twenty-two years developing the RAH-66 Comanche and cancelled it in February 2004, two months before initial operational capability, because the wars it had been designed to fight had already started without it. The Comanche was built for a peer-competitor conflict with defined front lines and massed armor. The Army in 2004 was fighting counterinsurgency in Fallujah. Its technologies were redistributed to Apache and Black Hawk upgrades. The technology worked. The war changed the question.
Where decades of incremental improvement had hit a wall, the Sikorsky S-97 Raider broke through it. Conventional military helicopters reach a practical speed ceiling near one hundred and seventy knots because the retreating blade stalls at higher airspeeds — a constraint limiting rotorcraft for sixty years. The Raider's rigid coaxial counter-rotating rotors eliminate the problem, and in testing it exceeded two hundred and twenty knots in level flight. The underlying patent had been filed by Sikorsky in 1946. The idea was seventy years old. The materials took that long to catch up.
No rotorcraft contract in U.S. history was larger than the one the Bell V-280 Valor won in December 2022 — the Future Long Range Assault Aircraft competition, with production estimates above seventy billion dollars. The V-280 cruises at two hundred and eighty knots against the Black Hawk's one hundred and eighty, uses around twenty-five percent less fuel, and carries the same payload over twice the range. Initial operational capability is planned for 2030. The Black Hawk it replaces served forty-five years. The Valor has to earn that.
Where the V-280 Valor won the Army's assault aircraft competition, the Sikorsky SB-1 Defiant lost it — but the rigid coaxial rotor technology the Defiant demonstrated is the foundation for Sikorsky's Raider X, competing for the Future Attack Reconnaissance Aircraft contract. The design that lost the largest rotorcraft competition in U.S. history may yet equip more American military aircraft than the one that won it. Procurement outcomes and technology outcomes are not always the same thing. It lost the contract. It won the argument.
The Boeing MQ-8 Fire Scout was among the first rotary-wing unmanned aircraft to conduct armed strikes launched from a surface combatant at sea, operating over Libya in 2011 and demonstrating that helicopter aviation does not require a pilot. Around seventy-four have been built. It operates autonomously from destroyer and frigate decks, carrying out surveillance and strike missions on a ship's schedule rather than a pilot's endurance limit. No crew required. It flew without a pilot. The implications are still landing.
In October 2022, a standard Black Hawk flew a thirty-minute fully autonomous combat simulation at Fort Campbell — nap-of-the-earth flight, obstacle avoidance, unprepared landing — with no pilot aboard, using the MATRIX system Sikorsky has offered as a retrofit for every UH-60 currently in service. The first aircraft on this list rescued one man from a jungle clearing in Burma in 1944. This one flew thirty minutes over Kentucky with no one aboard. The helicopter that defined an era just redefined it again.
